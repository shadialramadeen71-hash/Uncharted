On November fifth, two thousand nine, a U.S. Army officer opened fire on his fellow soldiers inside one of America's largest military bases. In about ten minutes, thirteen people were killed and more than thirty were wounded. The gunman was a psychiatrist. A man trained to heal soldiers. This is the story of Major Nidal Malik Hasan: what happened at Fort Hood, how he was stopped, and why his case is still making headlines today.

Fort Hood sits in central Texas, near the city of Killeen, about an hour north of Austin. It is home to tens of thousands of soldiers, and in two thousand nine, it was one of the busiest deployment hubs for the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Nidal Hasan was born in nineteen seventy in Arlington, Virginia, to Palestinian immigrant parents. He enlisted in the Army in nineteen eighty-eight, and the Army paid for his medical education. He graduated from the military's medical school in two thousand three, then spent six years at Walter Reed Army Medical Center near Washington, D.C., training as a psychiatrist.

At Walter Reed, some supervisors and colleagues raised concerns about his performance and his views. Between December two thousand eight and June two thousand nine, he sent nearly twenty emails to Anwar al-Awlaki, a radical cleric based in Yemen. A joint terrorism task force reviewed some of those messages, but concluded they could be explained by his research. No further action was taken.

In July two thousand nine, Hasan was transferred to Fort Hood. He was scheduled to deploy to Afghanistan later that year. In the weeks before the attack, he bought a high-capacity FN Five-seven pistol from a gun store near the base.

On the afternoon of November fifth, at about one thirty, Hasan walked into the Soldier Readiness Processing Center, a building where troops completed medical checks before deploying overseas. The room was crowded with unarmed soldiers. He shouted Allahu Akbar, and opened fire.

Soldiers dove under desks and tried to help the wounded. Several people charged directly at the gunman. Captain John Gaffaney, a reservist, rushed toward him and was mortally wounded. Michael Cahill, a civilian physician assistant, charged with a chair, and was killed. Specialist Logan Burnett threw a folding table at him, and was shot.

Hasan then moved outside, still firing. Base civilian police arrived within minutes. Sergeant Kimberly Munley exchanged fire with him and was wounded in the leg. Then Sergeant Mark Todd confronted Hasan, ordered him to drop his weapon, and fired. Hasan fell. Todd kicked the pistol away and placed him in handcuffs. The attack was over.

Hasan had been shot multiple times. He survived, but was left paralyzed from the waist down. After months in a military hospital, he was held in the Bell County jail in Texas while he awaited trial.

Why was he arrested? Because he was the gunman. Dozens of witnesses identified him, and investigators found shell casings, his weapons, and evidence that he had planned the attack. Within days, the Army charged him with thirteen counts of premeditated murder. Weeks later, it added thirty-two counts of attempted murder.

His court martial finally began in August two thousand thirteen at Fort Hood. Hasan fired his lawyers and represented himself. He admitted that he was the shooter, and said he had switched sides, claiming he acted to protect insurgents in Afghanistan. On August twenty-third, a military jury found him guilty on all counts. Five days later, the jury sentenced him to death.

Hasan was sent to the military's death row at the U.S. Disciplinary Barracks in Fort Leavenworth, Kansas. The Pentagon at first classified the shooting as workplace violence, a decision that denied victims combat-related benefits. After years of pressure from survivors and families, Congress changed the rules, and in April two thousand fifteen the victims received the Purple Heart.

His appeals moved slowly. In twenty twenty-three, the military's highest court upheld his death sentence. In March twenty twenty-five, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear his case. In October twenty twenty-six, President Trump approved the execution. The Army has set the date for December third, twenty twenty-six, by firing squad at Fort Hood. It would be the first U.S. military execution since nineteen sixty-one. But Hasan can still file appeals in civilian federal court, which could delay it for years.

Behind the headlines are thirteen lives that ended that day, and a fourteenth: Private First Class Francheska Velez was pregnant when she was killed. Their names are remembered on a memorial at Fort Hood.
